Materials, and what happens afterwards

DSGN 312 · Week 4 · Read this before the session — about 30 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

First: the number behind last week's failure

Some of your boxes would not close, and one of the causes had no name yet.

Board has thickness. A panel cut to the exact inside dimension has to travel around its own material, and it comes up short by however many thicknesses it crosses. Draw a box for a 60 mm product in 0.4 mm board and 60 mm across is not enough.

That thickness is called caliper, and it is a number you specify. This is why the material decision comes before the drawing rather than after it — you cannot draw a dieline correctly without knowing what it will be made of.

Grammage and caliper are different measurements

Students use them interchangeably and they answer different questions.

Measures Tells you Units
Grammage Mass per area Stiffness — how it behaves under load gsm
Caliper Thickness The dimension your dieline must allow for mm or microns

They correlate loosely and not reliably: a bulky, uncoated board and a dense, coated one at the same grammage have different calipers. Specifying grammage alone is not enough to draw with.

Substrates, briefly

What it is Where it goes wrong
Folding boxboard The standard carton material. Layers of chemical and mechanical pulp Bulky at high grammage; the reverse side is often grey
Solid bleached board White throughout, clean print surface Expensive. Reads clinical, which is sometimes wrong
Corrugated Fluted layer between two liners Strong, thick, hard to print finely. Caliper is large
Kraft Unbleached, brown, visible fibre Reads honest and natural — which is a claim, and a claim you may not be entitled to
Rigid plastics Moulded or thermoformed Excellent protection. Disposal is the whole problem
Compostables PLA, moulded fibre, cellulose films Only compost industrially, in facilities most areas do not have

Where it actually goes

This is the heart of the week, and it is where most sustainability claims fall apart.

A disposal stream is a route, not an adjective

Recyclable names no route. Recyclable where, in what stream, under what conditions? Your three copied recycling instructions will show you: most of them are conditional. Rinse. Separate the sleeve. Check locally. Those conditions are the whole subject.

The disqualifying feature

For every package that enters a stream, there is usually one thing that could knock it out.

The feature What it does
Gloss lamination A plastic film bonded to board. The board is now a composite and most paper streams reject it
The wrong adhesive Some cannot be separated in pulping and contaminate the batch
Metallised film Looks like foil, behaves like plastic, sorts as neither
A window Board plus a plastic panel. Recyclable only if the customer removes it, and most do not
Heavy ink coverage Can push a board out of some grades
Food residue The pizza box problem. Contaminated fibre is rejected

A designer who names the stream and the thing that threatens it has considered material. A designer who wrote eco-friendly kraft has stated a preference.

That distinction is what this week is assessed on.

How a claim is built

Not a moral topic this week — a structural one. Some sustainability claims are checkable and some are decorative, and the difference is visible in the sentence.

Decorative Checkable
"Made with recycled content" "80% post-consumer recycled fibre"
"Recyclable" "Recyclable in kerbside paper collection. Remove the window first"
"Eco-friendly" — there is no checkable version. The phrase asserts nothing
"Sustainably sourced" "FSC Mix, certificate number on the base"

The pattern: a checkable claim names a number, a stream, or a certificate. A decorative one names a feeling.

You will use this again in week 12, where it becomes the ethics lens and gets a judgment attached. This week you are only learning to tell them apart.

Choosing means rejecting

A material choice is only visible as reasoning when something loses.

"I specified 350 gsm folding boxboard."

That is an assertion. Nothing about it shows a decision was made.

"350 gsm folding boxboard. I rejected 300 gsm — at the 120 mm panel height it bowed under its own lid — and I rejected solid bleached board because it costs about 40% more for a print surface this design does not need."

That is a specification. Two things lost, and you can see why.

Your client will ask why not the cheaper one. "Because I chose this" is not an answer.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Substrate The material, named specifically. Not stock
Caliper Thickness. The dimension your dieline must allow for
Grammage Mass per area, in gsm. Tells you stiffness, not thickness
Disposal stream The named route the package actually enters. Recyclable is not one
Disqualifying feature The thing in your construction most likely to knock it out of that stream

Self-check

  1. Why can you not draw a dieline correctly without knowing the material? (First: the number)
  2. Two boards are both 300 gsm. Can you assume the same caliper? Why does it matter? (Grammage and caliper)
  3. Take your three copied recycling instructions. How many are conditional? What are the conditions? (A disposal stream is a route)
  4. Name three disqualifying features and what each does. (The disqualifying feature)
  5. Rewrite "made from eco-friendly materials" as a checkable claim. If you cannot without inventing facts, say what facts you would need. (How a claim is built)
  6. The package you brought that you believe is recyclable — find its disqualifying feature. Most have one.

Bring to the session

  • Three recycling instructions, copied word for word.
  • One package you believe is recyclable.
