Building it

DSGN 312 · Week 10 · Read this before the session — about 30 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

Paper told you about geometry. Board tells you about everything else.

Your week 9 paper model proved the panels add up. That was the arithmetic.

Board behaves differently in ways paper cannot warn you about:

  • It resists the fold, so a crease that was optional in paper is mandatory here
  • It has grain, so it folds cleanly one way and cracks the other
  • It is thick, so the allowance you calculated is finally load-bearing
  • It shows — a torn edge or a cracked crease is visible on the finished object

This is why the paper check came first and why it did not make this week redundant.

The order: cut, then crease

Cutting first keeps the sheet flat and stable. Creasing first makes the board curl, and every subsequent cut wanders.

Students do it in the wrong order by instinct. It costs a sheet.

Blades

A dull blade tears board. The edge comes out ragged and fibrous, and the student reads it as a fault in the material or the drawing.

It is neither. Change the blade. They are free and they are on the bench.

Two or three light passes, not one heavy one. Pushing hard makes the knife wander off the rule and it makes the cut worse, not faster.

Creasing

A crease is a compression, not a scratch and not a cut.

Bone folder against a metal rule, firm, single pass. The board is squashed along a line so it has somewhere to fold.

A fingernail is not a crease. It looks like one and the fold collapses unevenly, which is why the box will not sit square.

Grain and cracking

Board has a grain direction. Fold with it and the crease is clean. Fold against it and the outer surface can crack — and on a printed panel, the crack goes through the artwork.

Crease from the correct face: the material compresses on the inside of the fold. Crease from the wrong side and you are stretching the surface that has your design on it.

The handling test

Once it is assembled, you are not finished. 10.2 is a deliberate test, not an inspection.

Do all of these, in order, and narrate what you notice:

  1. Close it. Does it stay closed?
  2. Put the product in. Does it fit with the tolerance you allowed?
  3. Open it. Does it survive being opened?
  4. Close it again. Does it survive being opened twice?
  5. Set it down and pick it up. Does it hold its shape?
  6. Stand it as it would stand on a shelf. Does it stay standing?
  7. Invert it. Does the product fall out?

This is the last moment you find your own faults before somebody else finds them in week 11.

The 3D visualization must match what you built

Not flatter it. Correspondence is the requirement: same panel count, same proportions, same closure.

A render with cleaner proportions, a closure the box does not have, or a finish the board cannot take is a different design — and it will contradict the object sitting next to it in week 15.

Build the visualization from your dieline, then put it beside the physical mockup and check the three things.

About tape

If you had to tape it into shape, the dieline is wrong, and the fix belongs in the file.

Taping is not cheating — it tells you exactly what is wrong. What it is not is a solution. A second build from a corrected file is the right response and it is what the three sheets are for.

A repair made with tape is recorded as a repair, because a taped model photographs identically to a folded one and the observation is the only evidence either way.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Mockup The package built at full size in the specified material, assembled, holding the product. A physical object — not a render
3D visualization A digital image corresponding to the built object. Same panels, proportions, closure
Assembled unaided Built from the submitted dieline without tape, trimming or correction

Self-check

  1. Why cut before creasing? (The order)
  2. Your cut edge is ragged and fibrous. What is the cause? (Blades)
  3. What is the difference between a crease and a scratch, and why does it matter? (Creasing)
  4. Why can creasing from the wrong face destroy your artwork? (Grain and cracking)
  5. List the seven steps of the handling test. Which one do you expect your box to fail? (The handling test)
  6. Your render has a cleaner closure than your box. What have you produced? (The 3D visualization)

Bring to the session

  • The product.
  • The week 9 paper model, folded.
  • The final dieline file, checked against the board that arrived.
