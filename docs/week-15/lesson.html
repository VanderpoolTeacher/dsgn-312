DSGN 312 · Week 15 · Read this before the session — about 45 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

What is actually being scored

The account. Not the work.

Whether your mockup closes was settled in week 10. Whether your dieline folded was settled in week 9. Those marks are in.

This week scores whether you can account for what you made — whether the design's history is real, and whether you hold the reasoning or have memorised it.

This matters for how you prepare. A polished deck about a thin project does not rescue the project, and a brilliant object presented badly does not rescue the presentation. They are scored on separate rubrics and they are not averaged, deliberately: a student who builds well and cannot explain it, or explains beautifully over thin work, is telling us something true, and averaging destroys exactly that signal.

Bring the object

All of the mockups, product included.

The room handles them during and after. That is not ceremony — the account is tested against the thing. When you say the closure works one-handed, somebody is holding it.

A presentation delivered to slides alone moves the assessment onto the deck, which is the thing the separate rubric exists to prevent.

The shape, in about eight minutes

  1. The problem — the product, the audience, and what that audience forced
  2. What you specified and why — the substrate, and the alternative that lost
  3. Where the design changed and what caused it — your three dated states, shown, not described
  4. How the sustainability claim holds — including where it does not

Three dated states must appear on screen. Not summarised — shown, with dates.

Then the production files, opened live, briefly, with you naming which requirement two or three settings answer.

The three standing questions

Everyone gets these. They are published because surprise is not what is being measured.

1 · Why this material and not the one you rejected?

You made this decision in week 7 and defended it in week 13. The answer is not "it was better" — it is what the alternative would have cost, at what span, for what print surface, into what stream.

2 · What did the AI get wrong?

From week 8. A specific, checkable failure — a flap with no slot, a finish that could not go on your substrate, a mark that does not exist. And what you did instead.

"It helped me brainstorm" fails this question as thoroughly in week 15 as it did in week 8.

3 · What would you change with another two weeks — and what would you leave alone?

"I'd polish it" is not an answer, and it is the most common one.

Name the thing you know is still wrong. You wrote it in week 14 as the weakest point in your claim. A student who names it — "the window is polypropylene and most people won't remove it; I'd solve it with a die-cut aperture" — has demonstrated the judgment this whole course was building.

The second half of the question matters too. Naming what you would leave alone shows you know what worked and why.

Volunteer the weakness

The rubric's Exceeds row turns on this: naming the weakest point in your sustainability claim before you are asked.

You are being told the answer in advance. It still requires nerve, because it feels like handing over ammunition.

It is the opposite. A claim with an acknowledged limit is credible. A claim with no limits is one nobody has interrogated, including you.

Questions are the assessment, not the formality

Roughly five minutes after each presentation, from the instructor and from the room, with your mockups in people's hands.

A prepared deck can carry a recited account all the way to the end. Only an unscripted question distinguishes reasoning you hold from reasoning you rehearsed — which is why the questioning is scored separately from the deck.

Rehearse once out loud, standing, without reading. Rehearsing from notes trains the wrong thing.

Self-check

  1. What is being scored this week, and what was scored in week 10? (What is actually being scored)
  2. Answer question 1 out loud, without notes. Did you name what the alternative would have cost? (The three standing questions)
  3. Answer question 2. Is your failure specific enough that somebody could check it? (Question 2)
  4. Answer question 3. Did you say "polish"? Try again. (Question 3)
  5. What is the weakest point in your sustainability claim? Say it in one sentence, with what you would do about it. (Volunteer the weakness)
  6. Are your three dated states on screen, with dates visible? (The shape)

Bring to the session

  • The physical mockups. All of them. Product included.
  • The production files, openable.
  • The case study, with three dated states on screen.
