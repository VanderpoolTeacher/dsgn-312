Assembling the account

DSGN 312 · Week 14 · Read this before the session — about 60 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

One session

Thanksgiving closes Wednesday and Friday. You get one hour, and it is the week before your final assessment.

Everything below already exists in your files. This week arranges it — and the arranging is the only thing an hour is enough for.

If your three dated states are not in one folder when you arrive, the hour will be spent finding them instead of using them.

A case study is not a diary

The instinct is chronological: first I did this, then I did that, then I did this other thing.

It buries the finding. A reader gets to the interesting part on page four, if they get there.

The structure that works:

  1. The problem — the product, the audience, the constraint
  2. The outcome — what you made. Show it early, in a good photograph
  3. The route — the three states, and what caused each change
  4. What is still wrong — the weakest remaining point

Problem and outcome first. Then the route, which is now interesting because the reader knows where it ends.

Causal, not chronological

This is 14.1, and it is one word's difference.

Diary: "I changed the closure to the short panel."

Case study: "Two of three testers tried to open it from the wrong end, so the closure moved to the short panel."

The second names what caused the change. That is the whole of iterative design as this course assesses it — a revision with no antecedent is a preference, and preferences are not evidence.

The strongest sentences in a case study usually come straight out of the week 11 test record, because it contains other people's behaviour. "Two of three testers" is more convincing than any amount of design reasoning.

The weakest remaining point

14.2, and it is the part students resist.

Every sustainability claim in this room has a weak point. An adhesive. A lamination you kept. A stream that depends on the local authority. A percentage nobody verified. A finish you wanted and could not have.

Naming it is not an admission — it is what makes the rest of the claim credible.

A student who says "fully recyclable, no issues" has either not looked or is hoping nobody asks. A student who says "recyclable in kerbside paper, but the window is polypropylene and most people will not remove it — that is the weakest part of my claim and I would solve it with a die-cut aperture if I had the tooling budget" has demonstrated the judgment the course is trying to build.

Week 15's rubric explicitly rewards volunteering this before being asked. It is the Exceeds row. You are being told the answer in advance because knowing it does not make it easy.

The photographs matter more than you think

Your case study is the artifact that outlives this course. It is what goes in your portfolio.

  • Plain ground, even light. A packaging photograph on a cluttered desk reads as a student exercise
  • One shot with the product in it — packaging without its product is an abstraction
  • One with a hand — scale is otherwise unreadable
  • One showing it open, because opening is half of what you designed
  • Legible at projection size. Check it on a screen, not on your phone

Self-check

  1. Are your three dated states in one folder, named and dated? If not, do that now — it is the difference between a working hour and a lost one. (One session)
  2. Why does problem-and-outcome-first beat chronological? (A case study is not a diary)
  3. Write your three transition sentences. Does each name a cause? (Causal, not chronological)
  4. What is the weakest point in your sustainability claim? Everyone has one. (The weakest remaining point)
  5. Look at your mockup photographs on a large screen. Are they legible? (The photographs)

Bring to the session

  • Three dated states, in one folder.
  • Two transition sentences, written.
  • Photographs, taken.
