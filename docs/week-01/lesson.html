What a package is doing

DSGN 312 · Week 1 · Read this before the session — about 20 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

A package is not a surface

You have spent two courses learning to organise a flat area. A page holds still, sits square to you, and is seen under conditions you can roughly predict.

A package does none of that. It is picked up at an angle you did not choose, in light you did not set, by somebody deciding in about two seconds whether to keep holding it. Before any of that it was filled, sealed, stacked on a pallet, driven several hundred miles, dropped at least once, and put on a shelf by somebody paid to do it quickly.

By the time your design is seen, the object has already survived a process that had no interest in it.

That is the shift this course asks you to make, and it is the reason week 1 is about opening things rather than designing them.

The four jobs

Every package does four things at once. They are not a checklist — they are four demands on the same object, and they compete.

1 · Protect

The product has to arrive intact. This sets the structure: how rigid the material is, whether there is an insert, how the closure holds under compression when six of them are stacked.

2 · Inform

Some of what a package says is your decision. Much of it is not. Ingredients, weight, dosage, warnings, allergens, barcodes, recycling marks, who made it — these are required information, they have legally defined positions and minimum sizes, and they take the space your design wanted.

3 · Sell

The package has to be chosen, usually from a row of competitors, usually in seconds, usually at a distance before it is ever held.

4 · Survive

Filling lines, transport, handling, shelving, and the customer's own home. A package that photographs beautifully and arrives scuffed has failed at something no photograph shows.

Where the jobs collide

This is the part worth understanding before the session, because it is what makes packaging a design problem rather than a decorating one.

The tension What it looks like
Protect against Sell A tamper-evident seal protects the product and makes it harder to open. A window sells the product and weakens the panel it is cut into.
Inform against Sell Required text is often the highest-contrast element on a pack because the law says it must be legible. It competes for exactly the space the brand wants.
Survive against Protect Thicker board protects better and costs more, ships heavier, and takes more material to landfill or recycling.
Sell against Survive Uncoated stock reads honest and expensive-in-a-quiet-way. It also marks with every fingerprint between the factory and the shelf.

A good package is not one that wins all four. It is one where you can say which you traded and why.

That sentence is the standard this whole course marks against. In week 4 it will be about materials, in week 12 about accessibility, in week 15 about your own project — but it is the same sentence.

Friction, and what counts as one

In the lab you will open a package and record where it resisted. The word this course uses is friction, and it has a narrow meaning.

A friction point is located in the object and reproducible by somebody else.

Compare these two records of the same moment:

"It was really annoying to open."

"The perforation runs across the grain of the board, so tearing it pulls into the printed face instead of along the line."

The first is a reaction. It tells you the student had a bad time and nothing about the package — hand the same carton to somebody else and you cannot check it.

The second is a finding. Somebody else can pick up the same package, look at the perforation, and confirm or contradict it.

The test is always the same: would another person, handed this object, find what you found?

Some real friction points

  • A tab that lifts but does not release, because the die-cut stops 2 mm short of the fold.
  • A film seal with no start point, so there is nowhere to get a fingernail in.
  • Two flaps glued when one would have held, so opening it destroys the panel that carries the reorder information.
  • A clamshell that requires scissors, sold to a customer who is standing up in a shop car park.
  • A resealable strip that seals once.

Notice that none of these is about how the package looks.

The moment of choosing

The other thing you will do this week is reconstruct a decision you already made.

Think of the last package you picked up in a shop when you had a genuine choice. Not the thing you always buy — a moment where two or three options were in front of you and you took one.

Now try to say what won it. Most people cannot, at first. The reasons they give — "it looked better quality," "I liked the design" — are conclusions with the reasoning removed.

The useful version names something specific about the object relative to the others: it was the only matte one in a gloss row, it was the only one where you could see the product, it was the tallest thing at eye level, it was the only one whose name you could read from where you were standing.

This is not a memory exercise. It is the first pass at something week 5 will make precise — that standing out is always against something, and naming what it is against is the whole of the analysis.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Package The whole system — what holds the product, what it ships and sells in, and the surface design on both. Not box: most packages are not boxes, and the habit narrows what you notice.
Primary packaging What the product is actually in: the container, wrapper, or bottle.
Secondary packaging What the primary sits in: the carton, sleeve, or carrier.
Friction A point where the package resisted the person opening it, located in the object and reproducible by somebody else. Not annoying — that is your reaction.

Self-check

Answer these before the session. If you cannot, re-read the section named.

  1. Name the four jobs, and one pair of them that compete. Give a concrete example of the collision. (The four jobs · Where the jobs collide)
  2. Here is a record: "The lid was stiff and hard to get off." Rewrite it as a friction point, inventing whatever physical detail you need. What did you have to add? (Friction)
  3. Why does this course insist that a friction point be reproducible by somebody else? (Friction)
  4. Take the package you are bringing. Without opening it, predict where it will resist. Write the prediction down. You will check it in the lab.
  5. What is the difference between "it looked better quality" and a statement about the object? (The moment of choosing)

Bring to the session

  • One package, still assembled, that you have not thrown away.
  • A second if you can spare it.
  • Your written prediction from self-check 4.
