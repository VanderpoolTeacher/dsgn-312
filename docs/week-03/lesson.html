Structure

DSGN 312 · Week 3 · Read this before the session — about 30 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

A box is a drawing with instructions

The carton you flattened is a single piece of board. Somebody drew it flat, and every fold in it was decided before it existed in three dimensions.

That drawing is a dieline. It carries two different instructions to a machine:

  • Cut — the blade goes through
  • Crease — the material is compressed so it folds cleanly along a line

They live on separate named layers, because a machine reads them literally. A crease placed on the cut layer becomes a cut, and the package falls apart on the production line.

This is the single technical idea of the week. Everything else follows from it.

The common forms, and what each is for

Every form is a trade. Naming the trade is what makes the vocabulary useful.

Form What it is The trade
Straight tuck box The standard carton. Flaps tuck into the body at both ends Cheap, flat-packs, re-closable. Weak under compression and the tuck wears out
Reverse tuck Same, but the two tucks open from opposite faces Cheaper to nest on the sheet — less waste. Slightly more awkward to open
Sleeve A band around something else Elegant, minimal material, prints beautifully. Protects nothing — it needs a tray or a primary underneath
Tray and lid Two pieces, one sits inside the other Strong, feels expensive, excellent for gifts. Two dielines, two sets of material, more cost
Clamshell Hinged, usually moulded Protects extremely well, visible product, tamper-evident. Widely hated — often needs scissors, and that is an accessibility failure
Crash-lock base A base that locks flat when the box is squared Fast to assemble, holds weight. More complex die, more waste

You are not being asked to memorise this table. You are being asked to notice that no form is best — each buys something by giving something up, which is the same sentence as week 1's four jobs.

Why boxes fail to close

You will draw a straight tuck box in the lab. Most of the room's first attempt will not close. There are four common causes and they are all in the drawing.

1 · The glue tab is on the wrong face

A tab has to land on a face that is available when it is reached in the assembly order. Fold the box in the sequence it is actually assembled and you will find that some faces are already covered by the time the tab arrives.

This is not a drawing decision. It is decided by the order of assembly, and the only way to check it is to walk the sequence.

2 · The panel is short by the thickness of the board

A panel cut to the exact inside dimension will not close around its own material. The board occupies space. A box drawn to fit a 60 mm product, in board that is 0.4 mm thick, needs more than 60 mm across — and how much more depends on how many thicknesses the panel has to travel over.

You will meet this failure in the lab without being told the number. Week 4 gives you the number — it is called caliper — and explains it. That order is deliberate: the number means nothing until you have watched a box refuse to close.

3 · The crease is in the wrong place, or is not a crease

A fold made without a crease collapses unevenly. The box will not sit square and the panels will bow. A fingernail is not a crease — it needs a bone folder against a metal rule.

4 · It printed at the wrong size

The most demoralising failure, because nothing about the drawing is wrong. A dieline printed fit to page is scaled by a few percent and every dimension is off.

Always verify the print before cutting. Put a rule on a known dimension. It takes ten seconds and it is the only failure on this list that teaches nothing.

What a good failure looks like

The point of the lab is not to produce a box. It is to produce a diagnosis.

❌ "It didn't work."

❌ "The printer messed it up." — after you verified the scale

✅ "The glue tab lands on the panel that is already folded down by the time you reach it, so there is nothing to stick it to."

✅ "The end flaps are each cut to 60 mm, the same as the box depth, so they meet exactly and there is nothing to tuck."

A glue tab on the wrong face and a panel short by its own thickness are different faults with different fixes. Naming which one you have is the whole assignment.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Dieline The flat drawing instructing a machine where to cut and crease, at full size, on separate named layers. Not a template — a template is filled in, a dieline is drawn
Cut A line the blade goes through
Crease A line the material is compressed along so it folds cleanly. Not a cut, and not a fold line
Glue tab A flap that adheres to another face. Where it can go is decided by assembly order

Self-check

  1. Why do cut and crease live on separate layers? What happens if they do not? (A box is a drawing)
  2. Name three structural forms and the trade each makes. (The common forms)
  3. A box will not close and the panels are exactly the product's dimensions. What is the likely cause? (Why boxes fail — 2)
  4. You verified the print scale and the box still will not close. What have you ruled out? (Why boxes fail — 4)
  5. Take your flattened carton. Find the glue tab. Which face does it land on, and at what point in assembly is that face still available? (Why boxes fail — 1)
