Audience, and judging other people's work

DSGN 312 · Week 6 · Read this before the session — about 30 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

Part one: an audience is a constraint generator

Why "young professionals" is not an audience

It rules nothing out. Every design decision remains available, which means the statement did no work.

An audience is useful exactly to the degree it forces decisions.

"Someone buying a gift they will not see opened."

Now things are decided. The interior has to look finished, because somebody else opens it. The closure has to be re-sealable, because the buyer may check it. The price has to be unreadable at a glance, because the recipient will look. Three decisions, from one sentence.

"Someone standing up, outdoors, holding a bag."

One hand available. No flat surface. Possibly gloves. The closure has to work one-handed and the package cannot need to be set down.

The test

Write the statement. Then name two design decisions it forces.

If you cannot name two, it is not an audience yet — it is a demographic bracket, and demographic brackets are what people write when they have not decided anything.

This is exactly what week 7 will ask of your own project, so the practice here is not academic.

Part two: the four lenses

Every critique in this course covers four angles. They are not a checklist to recite — each one finds things the others cannot, and a critique strong on one and silent on another has covered a quarter of the job.

Market relevance

Does it belong on the shelf it is on, at the price it implies, against the competitors actually beside it? A beautiful package aimed at the wrong shelf fails commercially however well made it is.

Ethical considerations

Does it claim something it does not deliver? Week 4 gave you the vocabulary — checkable claims name a number, a stream, or a certificate; decorative ones name a feeling. This week you attach a judgment to that distinction.

Also: what does the package encourage? Excess portion, unnecessary material, a refill that costs more than the original.

Accessibility

Can the person who most needs the information get it? Can it be opened with limited grip, one hand, poor vision, or in a hurry?

This is the lens students are reliably thinnest on, because it is unfamiliar and because it is invisible if you are not looking for it. It is not "large type." It is:

  • Dosage or allergen information reversed out of a mid-tone at 6 pt
  • A seal that requires two working hands and opposing force
  • Variants distinguished only by colour
  • A closure that needs a pincer grip
  • Information printed across a fold or a curve

Visual communication

Week 5's hierarchy, now evaluated rather than described. Does the first read deliver what it should? Is the order the one the designer intended?

Part three: fault to change

This is the hardest thing in the module and it is what separates a critique from a description.

Description: "The type is small and the palette is muted."

Critique: "The dosage is 6 pt reversed out of a mid-tone, so the person who most needs it cannot read it. It moves to the flat panel at 10 pt minimum and loses the reverse."

The second names a fault, says who it harms, and says what would have to change. Something has to happen next.

The pattern

  1. Name the fault — specifically, in the object
  2. Say who or what it costs — the reader, the buyer, the claim, the shelf
  3. Say what would change

You do not have to be able to fix everything

Some faults are real and the fix is genuinely hard — a structural problem that would cost the client a new tool, a legal requirement competing with a brand element where something has to lose.

Saying so is legitimate and it is honest. "This has to change and I do not think there is a cheap way to do it" is a critique. "It's fine" is not.

What is not legitimate is naming a fault and stopping.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Audience A description specific enough to rule something out and force at least two decisions. Not a demographic bracket
The four lenses Market relevance, ethics, accessibility, visual communication. Each must find something the others did not
Critique Judgment with a consequence attached: what is wrong, and what would have to change. Not feedback, not review

Self-check

  1. Write an audience statement for a product you own, then name two decisions it forces. If you cannot name two, rewrite it. (Part one)
  2. Name the four lenses and one thing each finds that the others cannot. (Part two)
  3. Find a package with a colour-only variant distinction. Why is that an accessibility failure and not just a preference? (Accessibility)
  4. Turn this into a critique: "the back panel is cluttered." (Part three)
  5. What is the difference between "I cannot see a cheap fix" and "it's fine"? (You do not have to fix everything)
  6. Read the week 12 criteria. Which lens do you expect to be your weakest? Write it down. It is stable, and knowing it is most of the fix.

Bring to the session

  • Your audience statement, with the two decisions it forces.
  • Your predicted weakest lens.
