Putting it in front of people

DSGN 312 · Week 11 · Read this before the session — about 20 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

You cannot test your own package, because you know how it opens

Everything that makes your package confusing is invisible to you. You designed the closure, so you know where it is. You wrote the hierarchy, so you read it in the intended order. You know what the product is.

A stranger knows none of that, and that is the only useful position from which to judge it.

This week is about getting out of your own way for twenty minutes.

The nine words that destroy a test

"So you lift this bit here to open it."

You will say something like this involuntarily. Almost everybody does. The moment you explain, the thing you were testing — whether the opening is obvious — has been removed, and you cannot get it back with that person.

Say nothing. Hand it over and be quiet. The silence will feel much longer than it is.

Record behaviour, not opinion

Opinions are cheap and mostly polite. People want to be encouraging, especially about something you clearly made.

Behaviour is evidence.

Opinion Behaviour
"It's nice." They turned it over twice before finding the opening
"Quite clear, I think." They read the variant name as the product name
"Easy enough to open." They used two hands and their teeth
"Feels premium." They put it down within three seconds

Watch the hands. Watch the pauses. What did they try first? Where did they hesitate? What did they turn over looking for? What did they misread?

A pause is data. Write it down.

The three questions, after the watching

Only once they have handled it:

  1. What do you think this is?
  2. Who is it for?
  3. What would you guess it costs?

Record the answers word for word. Same discipline as week 2 — a paraphrase drifts toward what you hoped to hear.

The covered-logo test, on your own work

Cover your mark and ask a second stranger the same three questions.

This is the direct test of your week 7 audience statement.

If the stranger cannot say who it is for, your audience statement never reached the design. It stayed in the document.

That is a hard result and it is the most useful one available this week. You have four weeks left, and knowing now is the difference between fixing it and presenting it.

One tester is an anecdote

If one person is confused, it might be that person. If two are confused in the same way, it is the package.

This is the step students skip because it takes longer, and it is what separates a finding from a story.

Converting a result into a change

11.3, and the discipline is specific: the change lands in the dieline or the artwork. Not in the photograph, not in the explanation, not in the presentation.

Result: Two of three testers tried to open it from the bottom.

❌ Not a revision: "I'll mention in the presentation that it opens from the top."

✅ A revision: The closure moves to the short panel, and the tuck flap gets a thumb notch. Dieline updated.

A revision with no antecedent is a preference. A result with no revision is a note.

You may also refuse a change, with a reason

Not every result demands action. "One tester expected a window; I am not adding one, because the product is light-sensitive and the material claim depends on a single substrate" is a legitimate response — it names the result, and it names why nothing changed.

What is not legitimate is not noticing.

Keep the pre-revision state, dated

Week 14 needs three dated states. This is the middle one.

Save a dated copy of the file before you change it. A student who overwrites has lost the evidence for iteration and cannot reconstruct it — the dates are what make it evidence rather than assertion.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Test result Something a person who did not make the package did. Behaviour, not opinion
Dated state A saved, dated version of the design at a point in its history
Cause What a revision responded to. A revision with no cause is a preference

Self-check

  1. Why can you not test your own package? (You cannot test your own)
  2. Write down what you are most likely to blurt out when you hand it over. Being able to predict it is most of not saying it. (The nine words)
  3. Turn "they said it was fine" into a behavioural observation. What would you have had to watch for? (Record behaviour)
  4. What does it mean if a stranger cannot say who your package is for? (The covered-logo test)
  5. Turn this result into a revision: "the tester could not tell which end opened." (Converting a result)
  6. Have you saved a dated copy of your current file? (Keep the pre-revision state)

Bring to the session

  • The built mockup, product inside.
  • Three sealed predictions.
  • The question you are afraid to have answered.
