DSGN 312 · Week 9 · Read this before the session — about 40 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

Everything you need is already decided

You measured the product in week 7. You specified the substrate and its caliper in week 7. You selected a structure in week 8.

This week converts those into a drawing. Nothing new is being chosen — which is why a vague week 7 hurts here rather than there.

The caliper allowance, properly

Week 4 gave you the number. This is where it enters the drawing.

A panel has to travel around the material it is made of.

Take a simple tuck box for a product 60 mm wide, in board 0.4 mm thick. The front panel is 60 mm. The side panel is the depth. But the back panel has to reach across the front, around two folds, and meet — and at every fold the board occupies space.

The rule of thumb: add roughly one caliper per fold the panel travels over, and more where a panel has to slide inside another.

Where Allowance
A panel wrapping one fold + ~1 caliper
A tuck flap sliding inside the body + ~2 caliper, or it will not enter
A tray that a lid fits over + ~2 caliper on each relevant dimension
Dust flaps Slightly under — they must not collide

These are starting points, not truths. The paper check is what turns them into the right number for your structure and your board.

Why tuck flaps are the usual casualty

A tuck flap cut to the exact internal depth meets the opposite panel edge-to-edge and has nothing to enter. It looks correct in the file and is unusable in the hand. This is the single most common failure in this week's drawings.

Assembly order decides where tabs go

Week 3 introduced this. Now it is your own structure.

Walk your box through assembly in your head, in order:

  1. Which fold happens first?
  2. What face does that cover?
  3. When the glue tab arrives, is the face it needs still exposed?

A tab that lands on a face already folded down has nothing to stick to. The drawing cannot show you this — only walking the sequence can.

Do it physically if you can: fold your week 3 box, or a paper mock, and watch which faces disappear and when.

Layers, and why they are a convention rather than a preference

Set them now, because week 13 exports from these layers and a printer reads them literally.

Layer Holds Never
CUT Every line the blade goes through Any crease, any artwork
CREASE Every line the board folds along Any cut
ARTWORK The surface design Any dieline geometry
DIELINE-REF Dimensions, labels, notes for you Anything that must print

A crease on the cut layer becomes a cut and the package falls apart on the line. A dieline reference left on the artwork layer prints.

Bleed through folds

The move most students find genuinely counter-intuitive.

On a page, bleed runs past the trim edge. On a folded package, artwork must also run past a crease into the neighbouring panel — because the fold is never perfectly placed, and the board's own thickness pulls the surface slightly around the corner.

Artwork that stops exactly at a crease leaves a white sliver along the fold.

The sliver is invisible in the file. It is obvious on the finished object, it reads as a manufacturing defect, and it is entirely avoidable.

Run any colour or image that meets a fold at least 3 mm past it, into the adjoining panel.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Caliper allowance The extra dimension a panel needs to travel around its own board

Self-check

  1. Your product's width is X and your caliper is Y. What is your back panel dimension? Show the working. (The caliper allowance)
  2. Why is a tuck flap cut to the exact internal depth unusable? (Why tuck flaps are the usual casualty)
  3. Walk your structure through assembly. At what point does the glue tab's face get covered — if it does? (Assembly order)
  4. What happens if a crease ends up on the cut layer? (Layers)
  5. Why must artwork run past a fold and not stop at it? (Bleed through folds)
  6. How many print rounds are you budgeting for? (If the answer is one, read the lab)
