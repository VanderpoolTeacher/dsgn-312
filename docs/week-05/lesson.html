Brand in three dimensions

DSGN 312 · Week 5 · Read this before the session — about 30 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

The logo is the smallest part of it

Ask somebody how they recognise a brand and they will name the logo. Ask them to recognise one without the logo and most of them still can — which means the logo was never doing the work alone.

This week you will cover the mark on a package and hand it to somebody who does not know what it is. What they say is the measure of how much identity is actually in the design.

Most students discover their brand recognition rests on exactly one thing, and it is usually a colour.

What carries identity when the mark is gone

Colour

The obvious one, and the one your written list will be full of. Worth noticing: it is rarely one colour. It is a relationship — a specific red against a specific off-white, in a specific proportion.

Structure

A silhouette you would know in a dark room. A closure that clicks a particular way. A bottle whose shoulder is unmistakable. Structure is the hardest identity to copy and the most expensive to establish, which is why the brands that have it guard it.

Material and finish

Uncoated board reads honest and small-batch. High-gloss reads clinical, mass-produced, hygienic. Foil reads expensive. Soft-touch lamination reads premium-electronics. None of this is written anywhere on the package, and all of it is read instantly.

Typography

Not the wordmark — the other type. The ingredients, the instructions, the small print. A brand that sets its legal text with the same care as its name is telling you something about itself.

Tone of copy

"Directions for use" and "how to use me" are the same information and different companies.

What is absent

Sometimes the strongest signal. The package that shows no product. The one with no photograph of a person. The one that does not state a price where every competitor does. Absence is a decision and it reads as one.

Claim and delivery

A package makes claims. Some are written; more are not.

Matte kraft board with a hand-set typeface and a small print run claims small-batch, local, made-by-people. Whether any of that is true is a separate question — the claim is made by the material and the typography before a word is read.

The gap between what a package claims and what the product delivers is where week 12's ethics lens will eventually live.

For now, just name it. This week you are auditing what is claimed, not judging whether the claim is honest. The judgment comes later and it has a rubric behind it by then.

What an audit is

An audit is not an opinion about a package. It is a comparison between two things: what you predicted, and what a stranger actually said.

That structure is what makes it checkable. Anyone can look at a package and have a view. An audit says I expected this, they said that, and here is the design decision that produced the difference.

Why the prediction has to be written first

If you form your view during the test, you will unconsciously fit it to what you heard. A prediction written beforehand lets you be wrong, and being wrong is the more useful result — it is the only way to find out that something you were sure about is not in the design.

This is the same instrument you used in week 1 with your friction prediction, and you will use it again in week 11 on your own work.

The covered-logo test, precisely

  1. Mask every occurrence of the mark and the name. Front, back, base, seal, and any embossing. One visible mark invalidates the whole test — the person is then recognising, not reading.
  2. Write your prediction. What will they say this is, who is it for, what does it cost?
  3. Ask three questions, in this order. What do you think this is? Who is it for? What would you guess it costs?
  4. Record the answers word for word. Not summarised. The exact words are the evidence, and a paraphrase quietly moves them toward what you expected.
  5. Compare.

One stranger is not a result

If your prediction matched exactly, that is one person agreeing with you. Ask a second. Agreement of one is not a finding — it is a coincidence you have not tested yet.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
The covered-logo test Masking every occurrence of the mark and asking somebody unfamiliar what the package is, who it is for, and what it costs.
Claim What a package asserts, in words or otherwise. Material and typography make claims before any text is read.
Audit A comparison between a written prediction and what a stranger actually said, plus the design decision that explains the gap. Not an opinion.

Self-check

  1. Name four carriers of brand identity other than the logo, and give a real example of each from packaging you own. (What carries identity)
  2. Why must the prediction be written before the test rather than after? (Why the prediction has to be written first)
  3. A package is matte kraft with hand-set type. What does it claim? Now: what would you need to know to say whether the claim is true? (Claim and delivery)
  4. Why is one stranger's agreement not a finding? (One stranger is not a result)
  5. Your three brands from the pre-class task — for each, is what you named a colour? If all three are, you have found this week's lesson early.

Bring to the session

  • Your three brands, written, with what you would recognise each by.
  • Nothing else. The packages are supplied — using your own would test what you remember rather than what the design does.
