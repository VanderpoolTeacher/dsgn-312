Week 5 Lab — Shelf comparison

DSGN 312 · Packaging Design · in the room

About 45 minutes. Not graded. Feeds this week's assignment.

What you need

  • Your shelf photograph, whole row, from about two metres
  • The diagramming sheet
  • A partner

If you could not get to a shop, take one of the curated shelf photographs from the front. Do not analyse a product web page — e-commerce strips the shelf, and the shelf is the subject.

Steps

1 · Choose two competing packages from your photograph. They must sit on the same shelf — competitors in the abstract are not comparable, because contrast is a relationship between things that are actually next to each other.

2 · Get a second photograph of each at arm's length. If you did not take one, use the curated set.

3 · Diagram the hierarchy on the distance shot. Number what is read first, second, third, directly on the image.

4 · Diagram it again on the close shot. The order usually changes. That change is the finding.

5 · Partner check — hand your distance shot to your partner and have them number it independently, without seeing your diagram.

6 · Compare.

Where you agree, the hierarchy is doing its job. Where you disagree, it is not — and that is a result worth recording, not a mistake to resolve. Do not talk each other into agreement.

7 · Write one sentence per package explaining the first read in terms of contrast.

Name what the contrast is against. "Bold" is not a property; "the only matte surface in a gloss row" is.

8 · Note where the two distances disagree, and say which read the package was designed for.

Diagramming, concretely

Number directly on the image, with a line to the element:

①  the colour block — half the front panel
②  the silhouette — taller and narrower than both neighbours
③  the product name
④  the variant
⑤  the claim strip along the base

Five is usually enough. If you cannot get past three at two metres, that is itself the finding — most packages have three things that survive distance and the rest is detail for the hand.

The disagreement is the point

Students treat step 6 as an error to be fixed. It is the most valuable thing in the lab.

A hierarchy two people read differently is a hierarchy that is not working. Noticing that is worth more than a tidy diagram, and it is the skill that makes week 6's visual-communication lens possible.

Before you leave

  • Four diagrams — two packages, two distances each
  • Your partner's independent numbering, and where it differed
  • Two contrast sentences, each naming what the contrast is against
  • One note on which distance each package was designed for
