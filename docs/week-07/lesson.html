The project starts

DSGN 312 · Week 7 · Read this before the session — about 40 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

What you are committing to

From this week the course has one project. You will design, build, test and produce a packaging system for a product you choose, and you will present it in week 15.

Read the student brief before the session — all eight phases. Not because you need to memorise it, but because you need to see that this week's decisions are not preliminary.

  • Week 9 draws a dieline to the dimensions you measure this week
  • Week 10 cuts it in the substrate you specify this week
  • Week 13 prepares production files for the material you chose this week

A vague decision now is a blocked week later, and the blockage arrives when there is no time to go back.

Choosing a product you can actually build for

It has to exist and it has to be measurable

Bring the object. Not a photograph. The single most common way this project fails at the start is a student who chose a product they do not have, cannot measure, or borrowed and returned.

If you cannot put callipers on it, week 9 has nothing to draw.

Good candidates

  • Regular geometry. A box, a bottle, a jar, a bar, a tube. Rectangular is easiest and there is no shame in easy — the assessment is on the packaging, not on the difficulty of the product.
  • Small. Something that fits on a desk. Large packages need material you will not have and time you cannot spare.
  • Stable. It should not deform when handled, or your dimensions change between weeks.

Poor candidates

  • Soft or irregular — a bag of coffee, a bunch of flowers, anything that settles
  • Something you do not own — you need it in week 10 to test the fit
  • Something enormous — a bicycle helmet is a real packaging problem and not this one
  • Something with no plausible retail context — you need a shelf to design against

If your product fails on the day

Spares are available in the room. Swapping in week 7 is normal and costs a conversation. Swapping in week 9 costs a fortnight. Take the spare.

The audience, again — and this time it is yours

Week 6 taught this on other people's products. The task has not changed:

An audience statement must rule something out and force at least two design decisions.

What has changed is that you are attached to this one. You will want to write something that keeps your options open, because closing options feels like losing. It is the opposite — an audience that forces nothing leaves you with no reason to prefer any design to any other, and you will feel that in week 9 as an inability to decide anything.

"Someone buying a gift they will not see opened" forces a finished interior, a re-sealable closure, an unobtrusive price.

"Eco-conscious millennials" forces nothing at all.

Write the statement, then write the two decisions underneath it. If you cannot name two, it is not an audience yet.

The specification, again — and this time you care

Week 4 asked you to specify a material for a supplied product. This week is the same task on your own.

That difference matters more than it sounds. In week 4 you had no stake, so the rejected alternative was easy to name honestly. This week you will have a material you want — because it feels right, or looks good, or you have seen it used — and the temptation is to reject a straw man so that your preference survives.

Reject something real. The alternative has to be one a reasonable person would have chosen.

You still need: substrate name, grammage or caliper, disposal stream, the disqualifying feature, one rejected alternative with what ruled it out, and the structural consequence.

Scope: what can actually be built

The requirement is primary and secondary packaging. A container and an outer.

Students routinely propose more — a six-piece system with a custom insert, a printed inner wrap, a belly band, a hang tag. It reads as ambition. In week 10 it becomes an unfinished project, because building takes longer than drawing and you have three sheets of board.

Design something you can finish, then make it good. An excellent two-piece system beats a half-built five-piece one, and the rubric does not award points for count.

Vocabulary this week owns

Term What it means here
Brief Component A. Brand, product with measured dimensions, retail context, audience. The container everything hangs on
Specification Component B. Substrate, grammage or caliper, stream, rejected alternative, structural consequence
Retail context The named situation it is bought in — a shelf in a co-op, a delivery box, a table at a market. Not retail

Self-check

  1. Put callipers on your product. Can you state three dimensions? If not, bring a different product. (Choosing a product)
  2. Write your audience statement, then two decisions it forces. Cannot name two? Rewrite. (The audience)
  3. Name your substrate and the alternative you are rejecting. Is the alternative one a reasonable person would pick? (The specification)
  4. State your retail context as a situation, not a category. (Vocabulary)
  5. How many pieces are in your proposed system? More than two — what are you cutting? (Scope)

Bring to the session

  • The product. Physically. Three candidates if unsure.
  • The student brief, read.
  • Your draft audience statement and its two decisions.
