Compliance, accessibility, and the live critique

DSGN 312 · Week 12 · Read this before the session — about 30 minutes

Terms follow the Glossary.

What is happening this week

You will be handed a package you have not seen. You get a few minutes with it, alone, with no notes prepared and nobody to confer with. Then you critique it aloud across four lenses.

Nothing is submitted, and there is no second attempt.

That is not harshness. It is the outcome: critique packaging systems based on market relevance, ethical considerations, accessibility, and visual communication. The ability to look at unfamiliar work and say something true about it is the thing being measured, and any preparation time measures something else.

Week 6 had five minutes, a neighbour, and a second pass. This week has none of those.

Required information

Some of what a package says is not the designer's decision.

Depending on the category, a package may be legally required to carry: ingredients, allergens, net contents, nutritional information, dosage, warnings, a barcode, producer identity and address, country of origin, recycling marks, and batch or expiry codes.

It has defined positions and minimum sizes. The rules vary by category and jurisdiction, and the point is not to memorise them — it is to know they exist, that they are not negotiable, and that they take exactly the space the brand wanted.

Where compliance and design collide

The principal display panel is the face the buyer sees. Regulation often specifies what must appear there and how large. The brand wants the same real estate.

A designer who treats mandatory text as an inconvenience produces work that cannot ship. A designer who treats it as a design element produces work that does both jobs — the legal panel that is legible and well set is a real craft achievement and it is rarer than it should be.

Accessibility, concretely

This is the lens students are reliably thinnest on, in every cohort, including after week 6. It is unfamiliar, and it is invisible unless you are specifically looking.

It is not "large type." It is:

Reading

  • Required information reversed out of a mid-tone — white on mid-grey fails contrast badly
  • Dosage or allergen text at 6 pt when 10 pt was available
  • Information printed across a fold or around a curve, so it cannot be read flat
  • Variants distinguished only by colour — a person with colour blindness cannot tell the two products apart
  • Type over a photograph with variable tone behind it

Opening

  • A seal requiring two working hands and opposing force
  • A closure needing a pincer grip — arthritis, tremor, or one hand full
  • Packaging that needs scissors, sold to somebody standing outdoors
  • A tab so small it cannot be gripped with any strength

Handling

  • Weight distribution that makes the package tip
  • A shape that cannot be gripped with limited hand strength
  • No tactile differentiation between two similar products

The test for all of it: can the person who most needs the information get at it? Dosage information is needed most by people who are unwell. Allergen information is needed most by people for whom it is dangerous.

Ethics, with a judgment attached

Week 4 gave you the structure: a checkable claim names a number, a stream, or a certificate. A decorative one names a feeling.

This week you attach the judgment.

  • A claim the product does not deliver
  • "Recyclable" on a laminated board
  • A percentage omitted where its absence flatters
  • An invented or meaningless certification mark
  • Packaging encouraging excess — a large box for a small product, a refill costing more than the original

Stay on the object. "This company is greenwashing" is a verdict on a company. "'Made with recycled content' with no percentage, on a laminated board that will not be recycled — the claim goes or the lamination does" is a critique, and it is the one that scores.

Working four lenses in four minutes

You will not have long. A workable order:

Order Lens Why here
1 Accessibility Do it first. It is the one you will run out of time for
2 Ethics Read every claim on the pack
3 Visual communication Number the first read
4 Market relevance Where does it sit, at what price, against whom?

Accessibility goes first for exactly one reason: it is last on everyone's list by default, and what goes last is what does not happen.

Every fault carries a change

Unchanged since week 6, and it is what the coverage is scored against.

Description: "The dosage information is quite small."

Critique: "The dosage is 6 pt reversed out of a mid-tone. The person who most needs it is unwell and possibly reading it at night. It moves to the flat panel at 10 pt minimum and loses the reverse."

And you do not have to be able to fix everything. "This has to change and I do not see a cheap way" is a critique. "It's fine" is not.

Self-check

  1. Name six categories of required information. (Required information)
  2. Why is a colour-only variant distinction an accessibility failure? (Accessibility — reading)
  3. Name three opening failures that have nothing to do with type size. (Accessibility — opening)
  4. Turn "this company is greenwashing" into a critique of the object. (Ethics)
  5. Why does accessibility go first in four minutes? (Working four lenses)
  6. What was your weakest lens in week 6? It is stable. What are you doing about it in the next four minutes of your life that matter?

Bring to the session

  • Nothing. No notes, no reference material, no phone.
  • The package is supplied. That is the point.
