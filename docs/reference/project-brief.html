The Sustainable Packaging System — Student Brief

Step 3a of the Course Build Process · student-facing

What you're making

You're designing the packaging for a real product — the container it sits in, the box or sleeve it sells in, and the surface design on both. You'll build it at full size out of real board, put it in front of people who didn't make it, and change it based on what happens. At the end you hand over files a printer could run without calling you first.

Why this exists

Packaging is the one design object your audience holds. It has to survive a shelf, a delivery van, and a pair of hands, and it has to say what the brand is before anybody reads a word of it. By the end you'll be able to take a product, specify what it should be made of, draw a structure that folds, build it, test it on a stranger, and defend every choice — including the ones you made with a machine's help.

How this breaks down

Phase 1 — Pick the thing

Goal: Settle what you're packaging, who buys it, and where they find it.

Do this:

  1. Choose a product that physically exists and measure it. Width, depth, height, weight. Not "a candle" — this candle, 7 cm across.
  2. Choose or invent the brand behind it. Write three sentences on what it stands for.
  3. Name where it's bought. Not "retail" — a shelf in a co-op, a delivery box, a table at a market.
  4. Name who picks it up, in a sentence that rules something out.

Hand in: A one-page brief with the four items above.

You're done when: Your audience statement decides something. "Young professionals" rules nothing out. "Someone buying a gift they won't see opened" does — now the inside has to look finished and the price can't be the first thing you see. If nothing in your design has to change because of who you named, you haven't named anyone yet.

Watch out for: Picking a product with no dimensions. If you can't measure it, you can't draw a dieline for it, and Phase 4 will stop dead.

Phase 2 — Decide what it's made of

Goal: Specify a real material and prove you chose it.

Do this:

  1. Name the substrate, its weight or caliper, and where it ends up after the product is gone.
  2. Name one material you rejected and what ruled it out — cost, rigidity at the span you need, printability, disposal.
  3. Say what your choice forced structurally. Thin board needs a different closure than thick board.
  4. Decide what someone reads first, second, and third on the panel facing the buyer.

Hand in: The specification, one to two pages.

You're done when: Someone could order your material from a supplier using only what you wrote, and could say why the other option lost.

Watch out for: The word sustainable doing all the work. Which recycling stream does it actually go in, and what could knock it out of that stream? Gloss lamination and the wrong adhesive both can. Naming the threat is worth more than naming the virtue.

Phase 3 — Explore, including with AI

Goal: Generate more directions than you'll use, and find out where the machine is wrong.

Do this:

  1. Sketch at least three structural directions by hand. Folds, closures, how it opens.
  2. Generate packaging concepts with an AI tool. Save every prompt with its output.
  3. For each AI output, find something specifically wrong with it and write it down.
  4. Pick your direction and say why the others lost.

Hand in: Concepts, plus the AI log — prompts, outputs, and what each one got wrong.

You're done when: You've named a specific, checkable failure in an AI output. A structure that can't fold flat. A material that doesn't exist at that thickness. A finish that can't go on that substrate. A claim that couldn't legally appear on a label. Then what you did instead.

Watch out for: "It helped me brainstorm." That sentence is true of every tool ever made and gets you nothing here. Saying the output was all worthless gets you nothing either — that's not an evaluation, it's a shrug.

Phase 4 — Draw it

Goal: Produce a dieline that will actually fold.

Do this:

  1. Draw the dieline at full size, cut and crease on separate layers.
  2. Add glue tabs and check which face each one lands on.
  3. Allow for the thickness of your own board — a panel cut to the exact inside dimension won't close.
  4. Lay the surface artwork onto the dieline, with bleed carried through the folds.

Hand in: Dieline and flat artwork, native files plus exports.

You're done when: The panels add up. Every dimension traces back to the product you measured in Phase 1.

Watch out for: A dieline that looks perfect and doesn't close. Clean layers and tidy panels tell you nothing about whether a glue tab is on the wrong face. You find that out in Phase 5, and by then it's a re-draw — so check it now, on paper, cheaply.

Phase 5 — Build it

Goal: Cut, fold, and assemble the thing at full size.

Do this:

  1. Print your dieline at 100%. Check it with a ruler before you cut.
  2. Cut, crease, and assemble it in your actual material.
  3. Put the product in. Close it. Open it. Set it down and pick it up.
  4. Build the 3D visualization of the same piece.

Hand in: The physical mockup, plus the 3D visualization.

You're done when: It closes, holds the product, opens without tearing, and survives being handled. Your dieline, mockup, and 3D visualization all describe the same object — same panel count, same proportions, same closure.

Watch out for: Fixing it with tape. If you had to tape it into shape, the dieline is wrong and the fix belongs in the file, not on the model.

Phase 6 — Critique somebody else's

Goal: Look at unfamiliar packaging and say something true about it.

Do this:

  1. You'll be handed a packaging system you haven't seen. You get a few minutes with it.
  2. Say what's wrong with it — and what would have to change.
  3. Cover all four: does it work in its market, is it ethical, is it accessible, does it communicate.

Hand in: The critique, spoken, in the room.

You're done when: Every one of the four angles found something the others didn't, and each thing you named came with what should happen instead.

Watch out for: Describing instead of judging. "The type is small and the colours are muted" is a description. "The dosage is 6 pt reversed out of a mid-tone, so the person who most needs it can't read it — it moves to the flat panel at 10 pt and loses the reverse" is a critique, because something has to happen next.

This one can't be made up from home. It happens once, live, and there's nothing to submit afterwards. If you're going to miss it, say so beforehand.

Phase 7 — Change it

Goal: Put the system in front of people and revise on what you learn.

Do this:

  1. Show the built mockup to people who didn't make it. Watch them open it — don't explain it first.
  2. Ask what the brand is. Cover the logo when you do.
  3. Record what went wrong: where they hesitated, what they misread, what they couldn't open.
  4. Revise. Update the dieline and the artwork, not just the photos.

Hand in: The revised system — dieline, artwork, rebuilt mockup.

You're done when: At least one change traces to something a specific person did or said, and you can name what would have gone wrong if you'd left it.

Watch out for: Explaining the package before they touch it. Once you've told them how it opens, you can't find out whether it's obvious.

Phase 8 — Account for it

Goal: Present the work and leave with a portfolio piece.

Do this:

  1. Assemble a case study showing at least three dated states — early concept, revision, final — with what changed and what caused each change.
  2. Prepare the production files: correct dimensions, bleed through the folds, cut-and-crease on its own layer, right resolution and colour space.
  3. Present to the room with the mockups on the table for people to handle.

Hand in: Case study, production files, and the presentation.

You're done when: Each revision names the thing it responded to — a test result, a critique, a material limit. And a printer could run your files without phoning you.

Watch out for: Making the "early versions" at the end. Three states that differ only in colour are one design shown three times, and the dates give it away.

The presentation

You present in Phase 8, with your physical mockups available for people to pick up. Walk through the problem, what you specified and why, where the design changed and what caused it, and how the sustainability claim holds up. Expect to be asked: why this material and not the one you rejected, what the AI got wrong, and what you'd change with another two weeks.

It's scored on its own rubric — see the presentation rubric. Making the work and accounting for the work are separate skills, and a good talk won't carry thin artifacts.

Disclosure

Every submission carries a short AI statement — whether you used AI tools, how, and what you did to check the result.

Phase 3 is the exception, and it's the opposite. There you're required to generate with AI, and the prompts and rejected outputs are the deliverable, not a footnote.

Declare anything you didn't make: competitor packaging you photographed or reproduced, brand assets belonging to a real company, stock imagery on your surface design, and where your material data came from — caliper, recyclability, certifications. A sustainability claim with no source behind it is the thing Phase 2 is built to catch.
